The history of the printing press begins in the fifteenth century, when a goldsmith named Johannes Gutenberg assembled movable metal type, an oil-based ink, and a screw press borrowed from winemaking. Each of those pieces existed separately before him; the novelty was the combination, and the combination made books cheap enough that literacy could spread beyond the clergy and the universities. Within fifty years presses were operating in more than two hundred towns across Europe, and the number of books in circulation had grown from a few tens of thousands to somewhere between eight and twenty million. Printers experimented constantly with type design, paper, and page layout, and their experiments produced a new kind of standardized text: the same book, the same pages, the same pagination, in every copy. That standardization mattered as much as the volume, because it let scholars cite a page and trust that a reader a thousand miles away was looking at the same words. The press also changed how knowledge was corrected. An error in a manuscript could be fixed only by copying the whole book again; an error in a printed book could be fixed in the next impression, and the fix could travel with the type. Within a century the press had touched law, religion, science, and politics, and the Reformation, the scientific revolution, and the rise of the modern state are all hard to imagine without it. By the end of the sixteenth century Venice alone housed hundreds of printers, and the total European output of books had passed the one-hundred-million mark. Cheap print did not only spread old ideas; it created new ones, because an author could now reach readers he would never meet, and a reader could hold a position without belonging to the institution that produced it. The technology kept changing after Gutenberg: cheaper paper, faster presses, stereotyping, lithography, and eventually the rotary press all pushed the cost of a page down by another order of magnitude. Each improvement widened the audience, and by the nineteenth century a daily newspaper cost less than a loaf of bread. The press had become, in effect, the first mass medium, and the habits it created, skimming, quoting, arguing in print, persist in every medium that followed it.
